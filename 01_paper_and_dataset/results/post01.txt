🔬 #TransparentScience | Proper QC can uncover hidden treasures.

I’m back to one of my favourite topics: batch effects.

This time I went back to a 2019 Bioinformatics paper that asked a deceptively simple question: 
How well do RNA-seq differential-expression tools control false positives in a complex eukaryote?

It is an unusually attractive benchmarking dataset: 17 biological replicates of the same Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 genotype, generated in three experiments.

But while reprocessing the raw reads, several details caught my attention:
● The authors state that ERCC spike-ins were added to every sample and could verify comparability across experiments. I have not yet been able to locate the corresponding ERCC analysis in the deposited material.
● They excluded replicate 11 because its genome-wide expression correlation was substantially poorer than that of the other samples. Yet the supplementary QC shows substantial rRNA contamination elsewhere too—including replicate 6, which remained in the analysis.
● No pre-alignment read trimming/filtering is described. 

For differential-expression benchmarking, raw counts were passed to each tool and each tool applied its own default normalization; for the distribution analysis, libraries were instead randomly downsampled to equal depth.

None of these observations alone proves that the conclusions of the paper are wrong. They illustrate something more general: “high-quality RNA-seq dataset” is not a single property. Different QC problems can survive different filters, and apparently excellent global correlations do not guarantee the absence of systematic structure.

And there is one sentence in the paper that I particularly like:
☆ same lab, same machine, same people, same sequencing facility. ☆
Is that evidence that there was no batch effect? Really?!
